# UK Consumer Rights Summary

A plain-English summary written for the RetailOps Agent project, based on UK government guidance for businesses published on GOV.UK ("Accepting returns and giving refunds" and "Online and distance selling for businesses"). GOV.UK content is available under the Open Government Licence v3.0. This summary is not legal advice. It covers sales to consumers in the UK; sales between businesses are covered by different rules.

## 1. Which rules apply

### 1.1 Consumer and business sales
The consumer rules below apply when a business sells to an individual acting for purposes mainly outside their trade or business. When a business sells to another business (a trade or wholesale sale), the consumer cancellation rights and the Consumer Rights Act remedies don't apply in the same way. The contract terms agreed between the businesses, together with general sale of goods law, govern those sales.

### 1.2 The two main laws
Two sets of rules matter most for an online retailer. The Consumer Contracts (Information, Cancellation and Additional Charges) Regulations 2013 give consumers the right to cancel most online, phone and mail-order purchases, even when nothing is wrong with the goods. The Consumer Rights Act 2015 sets out what consumers can do when goods are faulty, not as described or not fit for purpose.

## 2. Cancelling online and distance purchases

### 2.1 The 14-day cancellation period
For goods bought online, by phone or by mail order, a consumer can cancel for any reason within 14 days. The period starts the day after the consumer receives the goods (for an order delivered in several parts, the day after the last part arrives). The consumer does not have to give a reason.

### 2.2 Returning goods after cancelling
After telling the business they are cancelling, the consumer has a further 14 days to send the goods back. The business can require the consumer to pay return postage, but only if it told them so before they bought.

### 2.3 Refund deadline after cancellation
The business must refund within 14 days of getting the goods back, or within 14 days of the consumer providing evidence that they sent them back, whichever is earlier. The refund must include the original standard delivery charge. If the consumer chose a more expensive delivery option, the business needs to refund only the cost of the cheapest standard option.

### 2.4 Deductions for handling
A business may reduce a cancellation refund if the goods have been handled more than was needed to establish their nature, characteristics and functioning (roughly, more than a customer could in a shop). A business may not charge a restocking fee to consumers who cancel within their rights.

### 2.5 Goods that can't be cancelled
The cancellation right doesn't apply to some goods, including: goods made to the consumer's specifications or clearly personalised; goods that deteriorate quickly, such as food; sealed goods that aren't suitable for return for health or hygiene reasons once unsealed; and sealed audio, video or software that has been unsealed.

### 2.6 If the business didn't explain cancellation rights
If a business fails to give the consumer the required information about their right to cancel, the cancellation period is extended by up to 12 months.

## 3. Faulty goods

### 3.1 The statutory standards
Goods must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described. These standards apply whether goods are bought in a shop or online, and a business cannot contract out of them with consumers.

### 3.2 The 30-day short-term right to reject
If goods don't meet the standards, the consumer can reject them and get a full refund within 30 days of taking ownership and delivery. No deduction for use is allowed during this 30-day period.

### 3.3 After 30 days: repair or replacement
After the first 30 days, the consumer must give the business one opportunity to repair or replace faulty goods before they can ask for a refund. If the repair or replacement fails, the consumer can ask for a price reduction or reject the goods for a refund (the final right to reject).

### 3.4 Deductions after six months
If the consumer rejects goods more than six months after delivery, the business may make a deduction from the refund for the use the consumer has had. No deduction is allowed within the first six months, except for motor vehicles.

### 3.5 Burden of proof
If a fault appears within six months of delivery, it is presumed to have been present at delivery unless the business proves otherwise. After six months, it is for the consumer to show that the fault was present at delivery.

### 3.6 Time limit for claims
A consumer can bring a claim about faulty goods for up to six years after purchase in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and five years after discovering the problem in Scotland. That doesn't mean goods must last six years; it's the time limit for bringing a claim.

## 4. Refund method and other points

### 4.1 How refunds are paid
Refunds should be made using the same payment method the consumer used, unless the consumer agrees otherwise. The business may not charge a fee for making the refund.

### 4.2 Goodwill policies
Many retailers offer returns policies that go beyond the law, such as a 30-day or extended Christmas returns period. These are allowed, but they must not mislead consumers about their statutory rights, and they don't reduce those rights.

### 4.3 Damaged in delivery
Goods remain the business's responsibility until the consumer, or someone they name, takes physical possession. Goods damaged in transit by the business's courier are therefore the business's responsibility.
